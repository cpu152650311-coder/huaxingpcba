PCB Minimum Order Quantity:
How MOQ, Panelization & NRE Drive Your Real Cost

Three different MOQs hide inside every PCB quote — here is how each one works, and how to lower your effective minimum without paying more per board.

Blog / PCB MOQ Guide

"What is your MOQ?" is usually the first question a buyer asks a PCB supplier — and it is the wrong question, because there is no single MOQ. A complete PCB order passes through three stages — fabrication, assembly, and component sourcing — and each one has its own minimum quantity logic. A factory that quotes "no MOQ" on bare boards may still hit you with component reel minimums that make a 50-piece assembly run expensive per unit.

This guide breaks down the three MOQs, shows the panel math that decides fabrication minimums, explains where NRE tooling costs hide, and gives six concrete strategies to lower your effective minimum order quantity. We run low-volume and prototype programs daily at Huaxing PCBA — 1 piece MOQ on both PCB and PCBA, with no minimum order value — so the numbers and levers below are the ones we use in real quotes. For the cost side of the same picture, pair this with our PCB cost drivers guide.

The Three MOQs You're Actually Dealing With

Each production stage has an independent minimum, and quotes often only state one of them. Know which one you are being quoted:

StageMOQ LogicTypical MinimumWhat Drives It
PCB fabricationPanel-based1 panel (5-40 boards)Panel size, board size, layer count
PCB assemblySetup-based1-5 boards (low-MOQ lines)Stencil, programming, line setup
Component sourcingReel/tape-based1 reel (3,000-10,000 pcs)Reel quantities, exotic parts

The fabrication minimum is nearly always the friendliest — a standard 500×600 mm panel holds a lot of small boards. The assembly minimum depends on whether the factory runs a dedicated low-volume line. The component minimum is where small-batch projects actually get expensive, because a microcontroller on a 5,000-piece reel costs the same whether you use 50 or 4,000 of them. How these three interact with setup charges is the subject of our NRE and tooling guide.

The Panel Math: Why Fabrication MOQ Is Measured in Panels, Not Boards

PCB fabrication is a panel process. The factory exposes, etches, and drills a large panel — commonly 500×600 mm (about 21×24 inches) — and cuts individual boards from it. So the fabrication MOQ is "one panel," and the number of boards you get from that panel depends on your board's footprint and how efficiently it nests.

1

Calculate boards per panel before you ask about MOQ

A 100×100 mm board fits roughly 12-15 copies on a 500×600 mm panel with tooling rails and spacing. A 50×50 mm board fits 60+. So "one panel" might mean 12 boards or 60 boards depending on your design — the MOQ in boards is a consequence of panelization, not a business decision. Panel layout rules that maximize this count are covered in our panelization guide.

2

Odd quantities cost more than the extra boards

Ordering 17 boards when the panel holds 15 means two panels, two setups, and twice the tooling touch time. Rounding up to a full panel is usually cheaper per board than asking for an exact count. This is the single most common misunderstanding in small-quantity quoting.

3

Multi-board designs share the panel for free

If you have several board variants for one product, putting them on a single panel — sometimes called a multi-up panel — spreads the panel cost across all variants and can drop each variant's effective MOQ. It also saves assembly setup if the variants share a process. Our prototype vs production breakdown shows how this shifts unit economics.

Component MOQs: Where Reels Make Small Batches Expensive

The quiet killer of small-batch PCBA economics is components, not boards. Surface-mount parts are supplied on reels and tape: common passives come on reels of 5,000-10,000, many ICs on 3,000-4,000, and specialty parts on shorter reels or tubes. When you order 50 assembled boards, your CM either buys full reels (and carries the surplus) or sources partial reels at a premium.

1

Turnkey assembly shifts reel risk to the CM

In a turnkey arrangement, the CM owns the component inventory. A factory that does high assembly volume carries reels of common parts continuously, so your 50-board order consumes from existing stock — the reel minimum never hits you directly. This is the strongest argument for turnkey assembly at low volume, and it is why consignment (buyer-supplied parts) can end up more expensive than it looks.

2

Exotic and long-lead parts are the real MOQ problem

A 1% precision resistor or a niche connector may only come in minimum buys far above your needs, with 8-16 week lead times. If your BOM has several such parts, they — not the PCB — set your effective minimum quantity. Component lifecycle and obsolescence planning is covered in our obsolescence management guide.

3

Standardize your BOM to shrink component minimums

Every unique value in your BOM is a separate reel or cut-tape purchase. Consolidating resistor values, using the same bypass cap across designs, and choosing distributor-stocked parts reduces the number of minimum buys. Designers who treat BOM standardization as a cost lever routinely cut small-batch unit cost by 10-20%.

NRE and Tooling: The Fixed Cost Layer

Beyond MOQ, every order carries one-time engineering costs (NRE) that are amortized across the quantity you buy. At low volumes, NRE per unit dominates; at high volumes, it vanishes. The three main NRE items for PCBA are stencils (typically $50-150 per board design), test fixtures (from a few hundred dollars for simple fixtures to several thousand for functional test rigs), and programming/line setup. The full breakdown, including which NRE charges are legitimate and which are padding, is in our NRE guide.

The interaction matters: a factory with a low MOQ but high NRE can cost you more at 100 units than a factory with a moderate MOQ and low NRE. Always compare total cost at your quantity, not MOQ alone. Our quote comparison guide shows how to normalize quotes for apples-to-apples pricing.

Six Ways to Lower Your Effective MOQ

1

Panel sharing and multi-up panels

Combine multiple board variants on one panel so the fabrication minimum covers several designs at once. Especially effective when the variants share layer count and finish.

2

Blanket orders with staggered releases

Commit to an annual volume (e.g. 1,000 units) with monthly releases of 50-100. The factory prices against the committed volume while you only take delivery of what you need — effectively splitting one large MOQ into manageable batches. The mechanics are in our blanket order vs spot buy guide.

3

VMI and consignment stock

Let the CM hold your finished goods or components under a vendor-managed inventory program. You pay as you draw stock, which converts a large one-time MOQ into a rolling drawdown. Our VMI guide covers the setup and the risks.

4

Standard parts, standard values

Design with distributor-stocked, reel-friendly components. Every exotic value you remove from the BOM removes a minimum-buy problem.

5

Prototype-to-production bridge at one factory

Running prototypes and volume at the same factory avoids re-qualification and lets the CM roll prototype learnings into the production setup — the fixed costs are paid once, not twice. The real cost gap between prototype and production runs is quantified in our cost breakdown.

6

Negotiate the effective MOQ, not the stated one

Ask what quantity makes the setup cost per board acceptable — factories with flexible low-volume lines can often meet you at 50-100 units with a modest setup surcharge instead of a hard minimum. The negotiation tactics that work with Chinese manufacturers are covered in our price negotiation guide.

When MOQ Shouldn't Matter: What a Low-MOQ Factory Looks Like

A factory that genuinely serves low-volume buyers is structurally different from one that merely tolerates small orders. Three signals: a dedicated low-volume or prototype line (so your 50 boards don't compete with a 50,000-board production run for machine time), component inventory depth (so reel minimums don't leak into your quote), and transparent NRE pricing (so fixed costs are visible, not buried in per-board price). Our low-volume PCB assembly guide explains how to verify these signals on an audit.

At Huaxing PCBA, the policy is simple: PCB and PCBA both start at 1 piece, there is no minimum order value, and first-time customers get free samples. Our 8 SMT lines include dedicated low-volume setups, and our component warehouse carries thousands of common parts on reels — so a 50-board turnkey order is a normal day, not a special case. That is the structural answer to MOQ: you don't need a factory that "waives" its MOQ for you; you need one whose economics don't require one.

Key Takeaway: MOQ is three different numbers hiding behind one word. Break quotes into fabrication panels, assembly setup, and component reels — then use panel sharing, blanket orders, and BOM standardization to shrink the effective minimum instead of hunting for a factory that claims to have none.
