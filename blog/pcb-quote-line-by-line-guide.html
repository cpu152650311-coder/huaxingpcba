A PCB quotation arrives in your inbox — 16 line items, a unit price, and a total. You forward it to your manager with "looks reasonable." But reasonable compared to what? PCB quotes contain layers of cost structure that experienced procurement teams learn to decode over years. The line that says "Tooling: $850" — is that one-time or per-order? "Surface Finish: Included" — included in what, exactly? "Testing: Flying Probe" — but does it cover 100% of nets or just a sample? This guide walks through every standard line item in a PCB quotation, what it should cost, what's negotiable, and the 5 red flags that signal an inflated quote.

A 20% markup on each of these can add $0.25/unit to a quote that looks competitive on bare-board price. Always ask for the test coverage report and the ENIG gold thickness spec.

Line Item #1: Tooling / NRE — The One-Time Cost Trap

Tooling is the most misunderstood line item in PCB procurement. It represents the fixed cost of preparing the factory to manufacture your specific design: generating drill and routing programs from your Gerber files, fabricating test fixtures (for bed-of-nails testing), and creating solder mask exposure artwork. Here's what you need to know:

1

Tooling Should Be a One-Time Charge — Verify It's Not Recurring

Some suppliers embed tooling costs into every order rather than charging it once. Look for language like "tooling amortization" or "engineering fee" on every invoice — that's tooling being charged repeatedly. A fair quote shows tooling as a single line item, charged once, with a note: "Tooling retained for 24 months for repeat orders." At Huaxing PCBA, we store tooling for 36 months at no additional cost. Our NRE cost guide covers tooling economics in depth.

2

Tooling Waiver Thresholds

At volumes above 10,000 units, tooling costs should be waived or deeply discounted. The supplier recovers tooling cost through unit margin over the production run. If a quote includes $650 tooling on a 50,000-unit order, that's $0.013/unit — immaterial to total cost and the supplier should absorb it as a relationship investment. Ask: "Can tooling be waived at this volume?" The answer tells you about the supplier's long-term orientation.

3

What Tooling Actually Costs the Factory

Factory cost for a 4-layer board's tooling — drill file generation (30 min engineer time), test fixture fabrication (materials + 2 hours), and photo tooling (films + exposure) — is approximately $180-300 in direct cost. The quote price of $350-650 includes overhead, margin, and a buffer for complexity. This means tooling is genuinely negotiable — you're negotiating margin, not cost recovery. 5 Red Flags That Signal an Inflated Quote

1

"Surface Finish: Included" Without Specifying Type or Thickness

ENIG at 0.05 μm gold thickness costs half what ENIG at 0.125 μm costs. If the quote says "ENIG included" without specifying the IPC-4552 class (Class 1 = 0.05 μm min; Class 2 = 0.08 μm; Class 3 = 0.125 μm), the supplier is likely quoting the cheapest grade while your design may require Class 2 or 3 for wire bonding or press-fit reliability. Surface finish selection should be explicitly specified in your RFQ, not left to supplier interpretation.

2

"Testing: Electrical Test" Without Net Coverage Percentage

"Electrical test included" without specifying coverage is the most common hidden cost in PCB quotes. Flying probe testing at 100% net coverage costs $0.08-0.18/unit. A "sample test" covering 10% of nets costs $0.02-0.04/unit. If the quote doesn't specify, you're almost certainly getting sample-only testing. For PCB testing methods comparison, flying probe 100% is the minimum acceptable standard for production boards.

3

Film / Artwork Charges in Addition to Tooling

Photoplotter film generation is part of tooling — it should never appear as a separate line item. If a quote has both "Tooling: $550" and "Film Charge: $120," you're paying for the same work twice. Ask the supplier to bundle it into tooling or remove it. This is a well-known industry practice in less transparent markets and an immediate trust signal — or warning — depending on how the supplier responds when challenged.

4

Impedance Control Premium on Non-Impedance Designs

If your design has no impedance requirements (no specified trace widths for 50 Ω or 100 Ω differential pairs), there should be zero impedance control line item. Some suppliers add it by default because most buyers don't question it. Check your design files: if there's no impedance coupon on the panel drawing and no controlled-impedance notes in the fab drawing, reject the adder. Our impedance control guide explains when it's actually needed.

5

Lead Time Surcharge Exceeding 15% of Unit Price

Expedited lead times (7 days instead of 14, or 3 days instead of 7) legitimately cost more — the factory must prioritize your job, potentially disrupting scheduled production. But an expedite surcharge above 15-20% of unit price is excessive. At 15%, a $3.80 board becomes $4.37. At 30%, it becomes $4.94 — and at that premium, you're better off adjusting your production schedule. Our lead time reduction guide covers how to get faster turns without paying surcharge premiums.

How to Request Quotes That Suppliers Can't Inflate

The best defense against inflated quotes is an RFQ that leaves no room for interpretation:

1

Specify the IPC Performance Class in the RFQ

IPC Class 2 and Class 3 have different annular ring, plating thickness, and inspection requirements. Class 3 PCBs cost 20-30% more than Class 2 because of tighter tolerances and mandatory microsection inspection. If you don't specify the class, suppliers default to Class 2 — which may be inadequate for aerospace, medical, or automotive applications. Our IPC Class 2 vs Class 3 guide helps you determine which class your product requires.

2

Request Itemized Quotes — Never Accept a Single "Unit Price"

A quote that says "4-layer PCB, Qty 5,000: $4.25/unit" with no breakdown is impossible to evaluate. Require all suppliers to provide the 16-line breakdown shown above. The suppliers who push back on itemization are almost certainly the ones with something to hide. Our quote comparison guide includes a supplier comparison spreadsheet template.

3

Include Volume Bands in the Initial RFQ

Instead of requesting a quote at a single quantity, request pricing at 3 volumes: 1,000 / 5,000 / 20,000. This reveals the supplier's cost structure — a supplier with high fixed costs will have a steeper volume discount curve; one with low fixed costs will have flatter pricing. You're not just comparing prices; you're comparing business models. Understanding cost structure gives you negotiation leverage.

Summary: Read the Quote, Don't Just Scan the Total

The difference between a well-negotiated PCB quote and an unexamined one is typically 12-25% of total cost — not because suppliers are dishonest, but because buyers don't know which line items to question. The three highest-impact actions you can take: always request itemized quotes, verify electrical test coverage is 100%, and confirm surface finish specifications (type + thickness) are explicitly stated. The suppliers who respond to these requests with detailed, transparent breakdowns are the ones you want as long-term partners. The ones who push back or offer vague "all-inclusive" pricing are signaling how they'll handle quality discussions down the road.
